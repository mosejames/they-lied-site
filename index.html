How we got here
Chapter 1 · The Meeting

A Sunday. A meeting room. 240 miles from home.

On June 28, 2026, at 4:10 p.m., the College Park City Council convened a Special Called Meeting in Savannah, Georgia — 240 miles from the city it governs. Present: Councilmembers Joe Carn, Jamelle McKenzie, Tracie Arnold, and Roderick Gay, City Attorney Winston Denmark, and City Manager Michael Hicks. Mayor Bianca Motley Broom was not there — she was presiding over a Georgia Municipal Association convention.

Fifteen College Park residents — Julie McGouirk, Elizabeth Lester, Hamlet Autman, and Jason Killip among them — drove four hours to be in the room. What they watched was a unanimous vote to award the city's Destination Marketing Organization contract to Destination Must Visit Tourism Alliance, Inc., a company incorporated in Maryland on May 26, 2026 — thirty-three days earlier.

The incumbent's contract expired two days later. On June 30, Senior Assistant Attorney General Kristen Settlemire sent the City Attorney a letter demanding an explanation within ten days, writing that the council's actions did not "foster public transparency."

The question everyone asked was why Savannah. That's the wrong question. The right question is: how long had this been in motion?

Chapter 2 · Two Tracks

What the public saw. What was being built.

For thirteen years, the ATL Airport District — led by Mercedes Miller and David Offenhauser — served as College Park's Destination Marketing Organization, funded by 1.5% of hotel-motel tax. Its most recent annual report claims $117.7 million brought into the local economy in fiscal year 2024-25 alone, and $678 million in total economic impact.

While that record was being built in public, a second track was running. Read them together.

The Ward 1 councilwoman places Jeremie inside the incumbent DMO's boardroom.

Jeremie incorporates "Destination College Park" — a competing DMO entity.
A sitting board member of the city's DMO forms her own destination-marketing organization for the same city.
FY 2024–2025
AAD reports $117.7M brought into the local economy.
Per its published annual report, AAD also committed $156,500 in incentives to secure $32 million in future business for the city.
Spring 2025
Destination College Park receives $104,000 from the city.
An $80,000 contract — $40,000 paid up front — plus a $24,000 performance bonus. McKenzie claimed sole responsibility for approving the bonus. The entity had no physical visitor center.
May 26, 2026
"Destination Must Visit Tourism Alliance, Inc." incorporates in Maryland.
The clock starts on the thirty-three days.
Jun 11, 2026
McKenzie demands AAD's detailed financial and operational records.
The request amounts to a complete operational blueprint for running a DMO.
Jun 15, 2026
Council rewrites the DMO rules and claws back ~$6 million.
An ordinance restricts DMO funding criteria in ways that exclude regional DMOs like AAD. A companion resolution reallocates roughly $6M in tourism funds — including $2M pre-committed to a "Roderick Gay Botanical Garden," named for the sitting councilman voting on it.
Jun 23, 2026
The Georgia Hotel & Lodging Association warns the city.
GHLA's Chris Hardman and Trent Nesbit put the city on notice: because College Park collects over $500,000 a year in hotel-motel taxes, state law (SB 127 / SB 430) prohibits reallocating restricted funds without approval.
Jun 25, 2026
City Manager recommends the 30-day-old company out of seven applicants.
Destination Must Visit Tourism Alliance is selected for recommendation two days before the Savannah meeting.
Jun 28, 2026 · Savannah
The vote. Unanimous. 240 miles from the people it affects.
Carn, McKenzie, Arnold, and Gay award the DMO contract. The Mayor is absent. Fifteen residents who drove four hours watch it happen.
Jun 30 – Jul 2, 2026
The state responds. The client list unravels.
The Attorney General's office demands an explanation. GHLA files a formal state complaint. SaportaReport reveals the winning vendor's claimed client list is disputed by the tourism organizations named on it.

Twenty-eight months separate the first private-track entry from the last. The Savannah meeting wasn't where the decision was made. It's where the decision surfaced.

Action recorded in the minutes

"And just for the record, City Attorney, I believe that's something within the guidelines that is allowed, is that correct?"
City Attorney Denmark
"Yes."
What it tells you
Twenty-eight months before Savannah, the same two council members — Carn moving, McKenzie seconding — put Carn inside the DMO structure, and Carn wanted legal cover on the record for doing it. This exchange has not appeared in any press coverage of the DMO crisis. It is on page 70 of the February 5, 2024 verbatim minutes, published in the city's own March 4, 2024 agenda packet.
Receipt 2 · The pipeline · board seat to competing vendor
The sequence
2024: McKenzie appoints Karen Jeremie to the board of AAD — the incumbent DMO. October 2024: Jeremie incorporates "Destination College Park," her own DMO entity. Spring 2025: the city pays Destination College Park $104,000 — $80,000 contracted, $24,000 as a bonus McKenzie says she alone approved.
What it tells you
A person placed inside the incumbent's boardroom by a council member built a competitor from that seat, and city money followed. Whatever else is true, the city was funding a parallel DMO more than a year before it voted to replace the real one.
Receipt 3 · The client list · reported July 2, 2026

The company College Park's council chose — in Savannah, two days before the incumbent's contract expired — is publicly accused by named tourism executives of fabricating the very track record it was chosen on. The characterizations are theirs; the documents and the cease-and-desist are on the record.
